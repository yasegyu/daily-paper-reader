# SkillSpec: Consensus-Gated Agent Skill Evolution via Representation Specialization 

Huancheng Chen Xiaodi Sun Zhaoqiong Huang Shenyang Huang Shreya Singhal Jingwen Lu 

> Microsoft AI

Natural-language skills are textual procedural memories through which large language model (LLM) agents retain reusable task knowledge without updating model weights. Existing methods typically treat skills as either static artifacts or monolithic documents optimized using aggregate validation scores as feedback. However, representing a skill as a monolithic document restricts optimization to its textual content, without explicitly modeling the structure through which procedural knowledge is retrieved and executed. We identify a key distinction between learning what knowledge to retain and determining how to organize it: textual updates should first be validated through execution evidence, after which the retained knowledge should be structured according to its procedural dependencies and retrieval requirements. To this end, we introduce SkillSpec , a two-phase framework comprising consensus-gated evolution and representation specialization. In the consensus-gated phase, complementary editing intents generate complete candidate skills. An update is committed only when paired evaluations reach consensus, requiring sufficient overall improvement and non-negative aggregate paired gain in every repeated evaluation. In the specialization phase, signals of process and redundancy sensitivity derived from the full optimization trajectory, including accepted and rejected candidates, guide the selection of a flat , graph , or hybrid representation. The validated backbone remains immutable, while a derived artifact is initialized and specialized in the selected representation. Across six benchmarks and three target language models, SkillSpec improves average success rate over SkillOpt by 6.89% ,averaged across the three models. These results demonstrate that reliable skill evolution and representation specialization address complementary objectives: deciding what knowledge to retain and how to structure it for inference.

## 1 Introduction 

Large language model (LLM) agents increasingly use natural-language skills to retain and reuse task knowledge without updating model weights. A skill is a textual procedural memory that encodes reusable steps, task rules, tool-use guidance, answer formats, and error-recovery strategies while remaining easy to inspect and revise. However, a fixed skill cannot anticipate every task-specific failure: execution trajectories may reveal missing procedures, conflicting guidance, or underspecified constraints. Recent methods therefore treat prompts and skills as optimizable state [1–6], replacing one-shot construction with iterative revision informed by execution feedback. SkillOpt [6] takes an important step toward controlled skill evolution through bounded, validation-gated edits to a trainable skill document. Nevertheless, aggregate validation scores can obscure arXiv:2610.00704v1 [cs.LG] 30 Sep 2026 an edit’s behavioral effects: improvements on some examples may conceal regressions on others, and apparent gains may not persist across repeated evaluations. Existing optimizers also preserve a single-document representation, changing only its wording while leaving the organization of knowledge fixed. Flat representations can obscure procedural dependencies in sequential tasks, whereas unnecessary structure can introduce spurious dependencies and irrelevant context. Our key observation is that deciding what knowledge to retain and how to organize it are distinct optimization problems. The former requires reliable evidence that an edit improves behavior, whereas the latter requires evidence about procedural dependencies and interference. Addressing both in a single rewriting loop makes it difficult to attribute gains or choose an appropriate representation. We introduce SkillSpec , a two-phase framework that separates reliable content evolution from representation specialization. In the first phase, SkillSpec generates candidate skills under four complementary editing intents: repair , preserve , simplify , and rewrite . It compares each candidate with the current skill on the same validation instances. The consensus gate accepts an update only when it improves performance overall with non-negative aggregate paired gain in every repeated evaluation, while retaining evidence from rejected candidates to inform representation specialization. Building on the validated skill and the trajectory of candidate evaluations, the second phase estimates process and redundancy sensitivity, then uses these signals to select a 

flat , graph , or hybrid representation. Phase II preserves the validated backbone as an immutable source and specializes a derived artifact, allowing both its guidance and organization to evolve within the selected representation. We evaluate SkillSpec on six benchmarks, SearchQA [7], DocVQA [8], LiveMath [9], ALF-World [10], SpreadsheetBench [11], and OfficeQA [12], using three frontier language models. Across the six benchmarks, SkillSpec improves average success rate over the no-skill baseline by up to 24.59%. Compared with SkillOpt [6], SkillSpec yields gains of 11.63%, 5.92%, and 3.12% for GPT–4.1, GPT–5.4 Nano, and GPT–5.4, respectively, averaging 6.89% across the three models. These results show the largest gain on GPT–4.1, suggesting that explicit skill evolution and reorganization can remain valuable even when the underlying model is less capable. Our contributions can be summarized as follows: • We introduce consensus-gated skill evolution, which combines multi-intent candidate generation with paired validation to accept reliable content updates, reject edits that fail the aggregate gain thresholds, and retain evidence from rejected candidates to inform representation specialization. • We introduce trajectory-guided specialization, which uses Phase I evidence to select and optimize a flat , graph , or hybrid representation while preserving the validated backbone as an immutable source. • We evaluate SkillSpec across six benchmarks and three frontier language models, outperforming SkillOpt [6] by 6.89% on average across the three models. 

## 2 Related Work 

Prompt optimization. A growing line of work treats natural-language prompts as optimizable artifacts rather than static instructions. Search-based methods use task-level scores to optimize candidate instructions [1, 2], while DSPy compiles prompts and demonstrations for compound LM programs [3]. TextGrad [4] propagates natural-language feedback through a compound system, and GEPA [5] uses reflective trajectory feedback while retaining Pareto-efficient candidates over validation instances. These methods establish execution feedback as a practical optimization signal, but operate on prompts or LM programs rather than persistent agent skills.

Skill evolution. SkillOpt [6] extends iterative optimization from prompts to persistent skills, using execution trajectories to propose bounded revisions and held-out evaluations to select updates. SkillOpt-Lite [13] replaces tree-based candidate merging with direct revision of file-based trajectories while retaining an independent validation gate. Subsequent methods prioritize candidates with greater refinement potential [14], stabilize update directions and adapt edit budgets [15], or train skill editors directly from execution feedback [16–18]. Other work repairs weak initial skills [19] or supports long-horizon repository curation [20]. Methods in this line improve how skill content is generated, selected, and maintained within a predefined artifact schema. SkillSpec builds on feedback-driven skill evolution with multi-intent candidates and paired, repeated validation, then reuses outcomes from accepted and rejected candidates to inform representation selection. 

Skill representation. Prior work structures skills at different granularities. Skill-Pro [21] represents an executable skill through initiation, execution, and termination conditions. SkillX [22] builds a multi-level skill hierarchy and iteratively refines its knowledge base, while HyperSkill [23] organizes subtasks and reusable skills in an evolving hypergraph for retrieval and maintenance. Graph-of-Skills [24] instead constructs inter-skill dependency graphs for inference-time structural retrieval, and AIP [25] compiles prose skills into schema-validated execution graphs. Most closely, AutoRefine [26] selects among rules, skills, and bounded subagents by compiling trajectory-derived interventions according to runtime ownership. Unlike methods that adopt a predefined structure or select among artifact types, SkillSpec uses outcome telemetry from accepted and rejected Phase I candidates to select a flat , graph , or hybrid representation for a validated skill before specialization. 

## 3 SkillSpec: A Two-Phase Framework for Skill Evolution 

SkillSpec optimizes an external skill artifact A for a fixed policy model πθ without updating the model parameters θ. For an instance x, executing πθ conditioned on A produces a trajectory τx(A)and evaluator score s(πθ, x, A). Training trajectories provide evidence for candidate revisions, while held-out validation outcomes determine which revisions are retained, with the goal of 

3improving performance on unseen test instances. Given an initial textual artifact Φ 0, SkillSpec decomposes artifact optimization into content evolution and representation specialization. Phase I uses training and validation evidence to produce a validated textual backbone Φ ⋆ and an optimization trajectory Γ I containing candidate artifacts, execution evidence, paired gains, and validation decisions. Phase II estimates structural sensitivity from Γ I and selects a representation r⋆ ∈ { flat , graph , hybrid }. It then initializes a representation-specific artifact from Φ ⋆ and specializes it to obtain A⋆. Figure 1 illustrates the two-phase workflow and the artifacts passed from Phase I to Phase II. 

3.1 Phase I: Consensus-Gated Skill Evolution 

Phase I evolves Φ 0 over T optimization rounds. At each round, the policy model executes the current skill on a training batch. Successful and failed trajectories then guide candidate generation under complementary editing intents. Candidates are screened against the current skill using task-level paired gains, and only updates confirmed across multiple executions are committed. The final skill is returned as the validated backbone Φ ⋆.

Collecting execution evidence. At round t, the fixed policy model uses the current skill Φ t to solve a sampled training batch Bt ⊂ D tr . Phase I partitions the resulting trajectories into successful and failed executions according to their evaluator outcomes. These trajectories and the associated evaluator feedback form the execution evidence Et used to propose candidate updates. 

Multi-intent candidate generation. Given Et, the skill optimizer constructs candidate skills under the intent set U = {repair , preserve , simplify , rewrite }. Repair adds corrective guidance for recurring failures; preserve retains guidance supported by successful trajectories; simplify 

removes redundant or conflicting guidance; and rewrite restructures the skill when local edits are insufficient. Each intent yields a complete skill candidate rather than an isolated edit. Compatible changes from different intents can also be combined into an additional candidate. The resulting candidate pool is St = { ˜ϕt,j }j , where j indexes the candidates generated at round t. Keeping these candidates distinct allows Phase I to evaluate each update strategy independently rather than fold all proposed changes into a single rewrite.

Adaptive representation selection. Phase II selects among three structural representations: (a) flat forms a skill bank of independently retrievable units without an imposed execution order; (b) graph connects guidance nodes through explicit dependencies to encode the required execution order; and (c) hybrid combines an ordered core with independently retrievable auxiliary modules. Figure 2 illustrates these three structures. The selector first uses PSS ( ψ) to determine whether explicit ordering is warranted. For high-PSS profiles, RSS ( ρ) then distinguishes graph from hybrid . RSS measures outcome evidence for harm from interference , not the prevalence of redundant guidance itself. Low PSS therefore selects flat 

to avoid imposing unsupported order. High PSS selects an ordered representation: low RSS selects 

graph to encode dependencies directly, whereas high RSS selects hybrid to preserve an ordered core while modularizing redundancy-sensitive guidance. 4.1 Main Results 

Performance across models. SkillSpec achieves the highest average success rate for all three evaluated models: 60.41% on GPT–4.1, 48.05% on GPT–5.4 Nano, and 78.27% on GPT–5.4. These scores improve over the corresponding no-skill baselines by absolute margins of 19.93%, 19.10%, and 24.59%, respectively. They also exceed the strongest external baseline for each model by 9.57% on GPT–4.1 (SkillAdam), 3.27% on GPT–5.4 Nano (SkillOpt-lite), and 1.76% on GPT–5.4 (SkillOpt-lite). At the benchmark level, SkillSpec leads on SearchQA, SpreadsheetBench, OfficeQA, and LiveMath with GPT–4.1; is best or tied-best on OfficeQA, DocVQA, LiveMath, and ALFWorld with GPT–5.4 Nano; and leads on SearchQA, OfficeQA, LiveMath, and ALFWorld with GPT–5.4, where it also ranks second on SpreadsheetBench. Even before specialization, SkillSpec-I achieves a higher overall average than any external method when averaged across all three models. This result indicates that multi-intent optimization in Phase I already provides a strong foundation, while Phase II yields additional gains on average through representation-specific continued optimization. 

Paired specialization gain. Across the 18 paired model–benchmark settings, SkillSpec outperforms its exact SkillSpec-I backbone in 13, ties in one, and underperforms in four. Averaged within each model, Phase II yields absolute gains of +3 .52%, +0 .74%, and +2 .67% for GPT–4.1, GPT–5.4 Nano, and GPT–5.4, respectively; the average gain across all settings is +2 .31%. Since each pair shares the same starting checkpoint and provenance, these results capture the overall effect of Phase II, including both representation selection and continued optimization. 

4.2 Phase I Dynamics 

Figure 3 suggests that accepted edits shift from behavioral contract formation to local constraint refinement as the incumbent matures. Early trajectories are shaped primarily by rewrite , which accounts for 15 of 32 accepted updates across all three models; these accepted updates occur in five of the six benchmarks. The less frequent simplify has four accepted updates, all in rounds 1–2. Later gains increasingly come from targeted changes: four of seven accepted repair updates occur after round 4, while preserve appears mainly on LiveMath and the already strong GPT–4.1 DocVQA trajectory. 4.3 Representation Ablation 

For GPT–5.4 Nano, Table 3 reveals distinct Phase I sensitivity profiles across benchmarks. 

SearchQA and DocVQA share a low-PSS, high-RSS profile, indicating limited support for benefi-cial explicit ordering but evidence that reducing redundant or conflicting guidance is beneficial. 

OfficeQA , in contrast, involves retrieving relevant documents, extracting evidence, and performing calculations that depend on the extracted values. Unlike answering from supplied snippets in SearchQA or a document image in DocVQA, this workflow introduces dependencies between intermediate steps, consistent with its higher PSS and the use of Graph to encode their order. 

SpreadsheetBench , LiveMath , and ALFWorld share a high-PSS, high-RSS profile in the Phase I estimates reported in Table 3, motivating both process structure and interference control. Depen-dencies between spreadsheet operations, mathematical reasoning steps, or state-dependent actions in ALFWorld motivate an ordered core, while redundancy sensitivity motivates keeping auxiliary guidance modular. The Phase I, equal compute control continues textual skill optimization without representation conversion, using the same number of optimization epochs and training examples as each Phase II variant. Using only Phase I telemetry, SkillSpec selects the best-performing representation on five of the six benchmarks. On the remaining benchmark, DocVQA, it selects Flat, which trails Graph by just 0.53% (66.58% versus 67.11%). Across all six benchmarks, SkillSpec’s six-benchmark macro-average is 50.27%, above fixed Flat, Graph, and Hybrid by absolute margins of 3.36%, 2.36%, and 1.17%, respectively. All aggregates are computed from unrounded per-benchmark scores. The six-benchmark comparison supports adapting the representation to Phase I sensitivity rather than imposing a fixed structure, without consulting test outcomes during routing. 

## 5 Conclusion 

We introduced SkillSpec, a two-phase framework for improving LLM agents through skill optimiza-tion without updating model weights. Phase I uses consensus-gated evolution to refine reusable knowledge, while Phase II uses trajectory-derived sensitivity signals to select and optimize a flat, graph, or hybrid organization. Across six benchmarks, SkillSpec improves the average success rate over SkillOpt by absolute margins of 11.63%, 5.92%, and 3.12% on GPT–4.1, GPT–5.4 Nano, and GPT–5.4, respectively. These findings suggest that effective skill optimization requires attention not only to what knowledge an agent retains, but also to how that knowledge is structured for execution.

This section specifies the candidate-level annotation and fixed ridge-estimation procedure used for the sensitivity scores in Table 3. It complements Equations (6) and (7): the language model supplies evidence-grounded labels, while numerical code fits the outcome model and computes the scores.

The unit of annotation is a candidate’s edit relative to its round’s incumbent skill, not the benchmark as a whole. Each record is identified by an observation_id and linked to a unified skill diff. The diff-only annotation workspace exposes observation_id , candidate_diff , and 

diff_file ; numerical screening gains and candidate metadata are joined separately by the scorer.

The annotator is instructed not to use or infer benchmark, dataset, environment, or sample identity, and to ignore intent names, acceptance decisions, optimizer summaries, tool counts, and the outer interaction format. A process annotation requires an added or strengthened ordered prerequisite, state handoff, or conditional dependency in the solution procedure. Generic advice to “reason step by step” is insufficient. An interference annotation requires added guidance that is visibly duplicated, conflicting, irrelevant to the surrounding skill, or disproportionately overlong. A negative screening gain is not itself evidence of interference. The same four-level strength scale is applied independently to both labels, as detailed in Table 6. The permitted interference modes are duplicated , conflicting , irrelevant , and overlong ;more than one may apply. Removing redundant text does not automatically receive a positive interference label: the instruction concerns what the candidate adds. If the diff is missing or supplies no grounded evidence, the prompt requests zero strengths and an empty mode list. The following illustrates the output schema, not a scored experimental observation: 

> {"trajectory_observations": [ {"evidence_id": "<observation_id copied verbatim>", "evidence": "<specific evidence from the skill diff>", "process_structure_strength": 0, "interference_strength": 0, "interference_modes": [] }]}

There must be exactly one observation per source candidate, with a unique matching identifier and a nonempty evidence string. Both strengths must be integers from 0 to 3; nonempty interference modes require a positive interference strength. The annotator does not output PSS, RSS, confidence, or a representation choice. The validated annotation runner allows up to three generation attempts when output fails JSON or schema validation, rather than accepting malformed output or heuristically repairing it.

Annotations are joined to recorded candidate outcomes by identifier. A candidate enters the regression if and only if it changes the incumbent, passes patch validation, and has a recorded 

screen_net_gain . Zero-gain and negative-gain candidates remain eligible, including candidates rejected by the update gate. Unchanged or invalid candidates and candidates without a screening outcome are excluded. The estimator uses screening outcomes, not pooled confirmation gains or final test scores.

Each column is centered and divided by its population standard deviation; a zero standard deviation is replaced by one. The design matrix also includes an intercept and one-hot indicators for round and candidate profile, omitting one reference level for each categorical variable. There is no process–interference interaction term. With X denoting this design and y the recorded screening gains, the fitted coefficients are ̂

β = ( X⊤X + Λ) −1X⊤y, Λ = diag(0 , 1, . . . , 1) .

Thus the ridge penalty is one for every non-intercept coefficient, including categorical controls. The implementation solves the linear system directly rather than forming the inverse. To evaluate a contrast, the scorer sets all rows’ process and interference strengths to the probe values, recomputes their squares, and retains each row’s edit size and categorical controls. It applies the fitted standardization, averages the predictions, and computes the extrema in Equation (6). The full-sample fit yields ∆ P and ∆ R; the bootstrap yields the positive-contrast proportions. Sampling uses NumPy’s default_rng(42) over sorted round labels. All eligible candidates from a drawn round stay together; candidates are not independently resampled. The validated scoring runner checks that the full-sample fit and all 2,000 bootstrap fits succeed, rather than silently reporting a proportion over fewer fits. These resamples reuse existing round blocks and are not 2,000 independent experiments. PSS/RSS summarize fitted effect-sign stability, not the probability that one representation outperforms another.
